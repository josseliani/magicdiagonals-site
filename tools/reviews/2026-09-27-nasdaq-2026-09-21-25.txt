NASDAQ Pre-Market Range Sweep Indicator: Weekly Review Sep 21–25

How the NASDAQ Pre-Market Range Sweep indicator performed this week

This week, I want to look again at how the NASDAQ Pre-Market Range Sweep indicator worked in real market conditions — which signals worked, which ones didn’t, and how I managed them.

Before we start, one important reminder.

The Entry / Stop Loss / Take Profit markings are informational and are available only on the 1-minute chart.

On the 5-minute and 15-minute charts, the indicator shows only BUY and SELL signals after the required conditions are confirmed, including the sweep. All trading decisions are still yours.

The targets are there mainly to give you immediate visual context. If price is still relatively close to the New York pre-market range, the midpoint and then the opposite boundary can be logical targets. If price has already moved away from the range by more than the size of the range itself, I become more conservative and look first for a return to the nearest boundary.

### Monday, September 21
The BUY signal came around 30003, already inside the New York pre-market range.

At that point, there wasn’t enough distance left to the upper boundary for 1:1.

This is where additional information can be useful. You can look at volume, volume spikes, another indicator, or whatever additional confirmation is normally part of your setup.

For me, there was enough confirmation to take the trade, but for this review I only want to count +1R.

After 1R, I can protect the position and leave the rest running.

Price eventually went much, much further (more than 1:10), and there was no real reason to exit while that strong move continued. But I’m not going to look at a finished chart and count the entire move as the result.

A signal is not enough by itself. Look at the available space, R:R, and any additional confirmation you normally use before deciding whether the trade makes sense.

### Tuesday, September 22
Tuesday gave us a SELL signal around 30680.

Stop Loss: 30733

Price moved in the right direction, but it didn’t return into the range and didn’t even quite give us 1:1.

So, nothing to invent here.

Almost reaching the target is still not reaching the target. Tuesday is an honest −1R.

### Wednesday, September 23
Wednesday gave us a much better situation.

SELL Entry: 30685
Stop Loss: 30755

First, I can take 1:1 and protect the rest of the position.

But unlike Monday, this time I can clearly see what I want to use as the next target. Below us, we still have the untouched boundary of Tuesday’s range.

So I have a logical reason to keep part of the position running.

Final target: 30494

That gives us approximately +2.7R.

This is exactly why I like to keep previous ranges on the chart. A range doesn’t automatically become irrelevant when the day ends.

### Friday, September 25
Friday is a very good example to look at more closely.

The first BUY came around 30630.

Stop Loss: 30598
Take Profit: 30677

Price gave us 1R and reacted almost perfectly from the middle of the range. It moved down again, swept liquidity one more time, and then we received another BUY signal.

For the second trade, the entry was around 30530 and the target was the upper boundary of the range around 30624.5.

With a wider Stop Loss around 30439, the trade gives us approximately 1:1.

But NASDAQ is a volatile instrument, and I don’t always see a reason to keep such a large stop.

If I place the Stop Loss behind the latest low around 30505, the signal, entry and target remain the same — only the Stop Loss changes.

And now the same trade gives us more than 1:3.

The tighter Stop Loss was not touched.

Same signal. Same target. Different Stop Loss — completely different R:R.

Price continued higher afterwards, but there is no reason to keep adding the rest of that movement to our result. Our planned target had already been reached.

## One more thing to notice

Here is the overall picture on the 5-minute chart.

As the week develops, these ranges don’t have to disappear from your analysis. Their boundaries can continue to work as important liquidity zones on the higher timeframe.

After Monday, we already have our first levels. Tuesday adds another range, then Wednesday adds another one, and so on.

By the end of the week, you are not looking at five completely separate trading days anymore. You already have a map of liquidity zones created during the week — and price can come back to them later.
